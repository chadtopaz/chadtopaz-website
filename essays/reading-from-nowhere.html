Reading from nowhere

Ernest Davis’s review gets several of my arguments wrong and overlooks evidence relevant to his conclusions.

September 2026

Ernest Davis reviewed Unlocking Justice in the September issue of SIAM News. He called my positivity “rather wan,” and wrote that nothing in the book justifies its subtitle, since there is “little indication” my work has changed anything.

He calls the passages on data acquisition the most technically interesting part of the book, and I agree. I was also glad he noticed the actions suggested for readers at the end of each core chapter.

I want to take up four of his claims.

OneGalton

Davis writes that I worry Francis Galton’s statistical methods are “irremediably tainted” by his racism and eugenicism. Here is the passage he is describing, at the end of the policing chapter:

As for the second QuantCrit moment, it’s easy to view regression as just another math tool, but we must not forget its roots in the harmful ideology of eugenics. Francis Galton sought to prove the superiority of certain racial groups — principally, white people of European origin — and promote ideas of selective breeding. While this history doesn’t invalidate modern regression techniques, it offers a stark reminder of their potential misuse, a legacy that’s all too easily overlooked.

Unlocking Justice, pp. 62–63

The passage says plainly that the history does not invalidate the method. Davis’s paraphrase leaves that qualification out.

Galton comes up earlier as well, in Chapter 2’s account of the eugenics movement (pp. 33–35). It faults eugenicists for treating correlation as causation, for drawing samples from prisons and asylums and generalizing to whole populations, and for collapsing people into a single distorted measure of “fitness.”

TwoObjectivity

Davis writes that I do not worry “that perhaps [I] might be mistaken in assuming that anyone who claims that mathematical truths are objective is in fact motivated by the wish to preserve the established power structure.”

The review cites no passage for that, and I cannot find one. Chapter 8 argues that when we treat math or data as objective, we “risk laundering bias as neutrality” (pp. 165–166). That passage attributes no motive to people who regard mathematics as objective.

The passage that most directly connects objectivity to power comes in Chapter 2, where I write that “those in power defined the data, called it objective, and then used it to argue for keeping others out” (p. 35). If Davis has this passage in mind, its subject is the eugenics movement, not everyone who thinks mathematics is objective.

ThreeThe mathematics

Davis writes that my book assumes a math-phobic reader, and that the math “rarely goes beyond elementary school arithmetic.” I wrote the calculations so that readers with little mathematical training could follow them. Chapter 4, on bail, asks readers whether to use the mean or the median when calculating total days in jail.

Our bail report estimated the number of additional people detained before trial because of a judge’s carcerality, meaning the judge’s propensity to keep defendants in jail. It then multiplied that estimate by the citywide mean detention length of 97 days, the average for defendants discharged in 2021. Twelve judicial groups objected in a public letter that 97 was a mean rather than a median, and asked why we assumed each additional person was held for that period. The former president of the New York State Bar Association pressed the same reading harder. On our logic, he wrote, a remanded defendant and one who posts bail at arraignment are both counted as detained 97 days, which he called absurd.

So the chapter asks the reader to work an example. Seven people await trial for 10, 20, 30, 40, 104, 110, and 365 days. The median is 40. The mean is 97. Multiplying the median by seven gives 280, which understates the total. Multiplying the mean by seven gives 679, which is the actual sum.

Using the mean in this calculation does not assume that all seven people spent the same number of days in jail, and multiplying the median by the number of people does not in general recover the total. Whether the citywide mean was the right multiplier for the additional group is a separate question, and the example does not settle it.

Chapter 3 fits a model of police call rates and compares its predictions against the observed counts. It then explains why the published analysis used Bayesian modeling. With so few census block groups in Williamstown, the observed counts alone left substantial uncertainty in the estimates, so we used prior information about plausible call rates to help constrain them.

Chapter 7 concerns a risk score that courts use to predict whether someone will reoffend. It gives the reader two definitions of a fair score (pp. 150–151). One says the score should flag the same share of each racial group as high risk, say 20 percent of both Black and white people. The other says the score’s predictions should be correct for the same share of each group, say 75 percent of the time for both. The chapter points out that the two goals can conflict, since equalizing the share flagged as high risk can leave accuracy unequal across groups, and equalizing accuracy can leave the shares unequal. The reader has to decide which requirement should govern the court’s use of the score.

FourChange

The subtitle sets a demanding standard. “Change is hard and takes time,” my last chapter says, and “the world doesn’t magically improve simply because you or I publish research or share data” (p. 187).

Davis discusses JUSTFAIR, our database that links 600,000 federal sentences handed down between 2001 and 2018 to the judges who imposed them. Before JUSTFAIR, the government’s public sentencing data did not identify the sentencing judges, and the scholarly work that had linked sentences to judges ran on proprietary data that forbade naming them. JUSTFAIR pulled that information together from several government sources, and it is free and public.

After JUSTFAIR went live, a couple of federal judges got in touch, wanting to examine their own sentencing patterns for bias because their court systems would not produce that analysis for them. My book calls it “one of the most optimism-inducing experiences of my professional life” (p. 140). I do not know what they did next.

JUSTFAIR changed what the public can examine. Whether it changes how judges sentence is a separate question, and my book does not claim to have settled it. The review acknowledges the database but does not explain how that new public access figures in its judgment about change.

The reviewer’s own standard

Michael Harris also objected to having a position attributed to him in Davis’s 2015 review of Mathematics without Apologies for SIAM News. Davis had quoted a sentence without its opening words, “It’s easy to argue that,” and without noting that it came from a dialogue between two fictional characters. Harris pointed out that the quotation turned a character’s hypothetical into his own position.

In a 2014 essay on how social scientists study conservatism and religion, Davis argued that researchers should approach beliefs they reject with an attitude of respect, presuming not that the belief is true but that it is not stupid. Otherwise, he warned, “Why do people believe X?” turns into “Why are people so stupid as to believe X?” I think that obligation applies to reviewers too, and it means giving an accurate account of the argument you criticize.

Chapter 2 of my book argues that nobody analyzes from nowhere, that the questions we ask and the evidence we credit depend on where we stand, and that stating our premises is what lets someone else check us (p. 33). Reading this review, I cannot tell what Davis would accept as evidence of change. I would also like him to identify the passage behind his claim about objectivity.

The review presents a book whose author worries that Galton’s methods are irremediably tainted and assumes that anyone who calls mathematical truths objective wants to preserve the established power structure.

I did not write that book.

Page references are to the first edition of Unlocking Justice (Princeton University Press, 2026). The review is Ernest Davis, “The Impact of Mathematics and Computation on Society,” SIAM News, Volume 59, Issue 7, September 1, 2026.
