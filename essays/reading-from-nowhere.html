Reading from nowhere

A review of my book misdescribed what it argues, and reached conclusions without addressing the evidence in it.

September 2026

Ernest Davis reviewed Unlocking Justice in the September issue of SIAM News. He called my positivity “rather wan,” and wrote that nothing in the book justifies its subtitle, since there is “little indication” my work has changed anything.

Authors who complain about reviews are tedious, so let me say what I am not doing. I am not arguing that Davis should have liked the book, or that he was too hard on it. A hostile reviewer who quotes me correctly is worth more to me than a friendly one who doesn’t.

He gets some things right. He picks out the passages on data acquisition as the most technically interesting material, and I agree. He also notices that every core chapter closes with concrete actions for readers, which is the part I most hoped a reviewer would notice. And I put “confront inequity and create change” on the cover, so asking what changed is fair game.

I want to take up four of his claims. One paraphrases a passage in my book and drops the sentence that qualifies it. One attributes to me a position the book does not contain. The other two are judgments he is entitled to make, but the review does not take up the evidence behind them.

OneGalton

Davis writes that I worry Francis Galton’s statistical methods are “irremediably tainted” by his racism and eugenicism. Here is the passage he is describing, at the end of the policing chapter:

As for the second QuantCrit moment, it’s easy to view regression as just another math tool, but we must not forget its roots in the harmful ideology of eugenics. Francis Galton sought to prove the superiority of certain racial groups — principally, white people of European origin — and promote ideas of selective breeding. While this history doesn’t invalidate modern regression techniques, it offers a stark reminder of their potential misuse, a legacy that’s all too easily overlooked.

Unlocking Justice, pp. 62–63

The passage asks readers to remember where regression came from, warns that it can be misused, and says plainly that none of this invalidates the method. Those are three separate things, and the third is the one the review omits.

Galton comes up earlier as well, in Chapter 2’s account of the eugenics movement (pp. 33–35). It faults eugenicists for treating correlation as causation, for drawing samples from prisons and asylums and generalizing to whole populations, and for collapsing people into a single distorted measure of “fitness.” Those are failures of causal inference, sampling, and measurement.

TwoObjectivity

Davis writes that I do not worry “that perhaps [I] might be mistaken in assuming that anyone who claims that mathematical truths are objective is in fact motivated by the wish to preserve the established power structure.”

The review cites no passage for that, and I cannot find one. Chapter 8 argues that when we treat math or data as objective, we “risk laundering bias as neutrality” (pp. 165–166). That describes what an assumption does. It says nothing about what anyone wants, and the chapter’s case for it runs through axioms, proof standards, and Gödel rather than through motives.

The passage that most directly connects objectivity to power is about a particular group of people. Writing on eugenics, I say that “those in power defined the data, called it objective, and then used it to argue for keeping others out” (p. 35). That sentence is about eugenicists. The review seems to read it as a claim about everyone who thinks mathematics is objective.

Davis has worked directly on the questions my chapter takes up. He co-edited Mathematics, Substance and Surmise (Springer, 2015), a collection on the meaning and ontology of mathematics. That is what makes the misattribution so puzzling.

ThreeThe mathematics

Davis writes that my book assumes a math-phobic reader, and that the math “rarely goes beyond elementary school arithmetic.” He is right that I wrote for readers wary of mathematics. That was the point. He is right about the calculations too. Those really are simple. The reasoning they support is not.

Chapter 4 is the clearest case. Our bail report estimated excess pretrial detention by multiplying the number of people we estimated were detained because of a judge’s carcerality by a citywide mean detention length of 97 days, the average for defendants discharged in 2021. Twelve judicial groups objected in a public letter that 97 was a mean rather than a median, and asked why we assumed each additional person was held for that period. The former president of the New York State Bar Association pressed the same reading harder: on our logic, he wrote, a remanded defendant and one who posts bail at arraignment are both counted as detained 97 days, which he called absurd.

So the chapter asks the reader to work an example. Seven people await trial for 10, 20, 30, 40, 104, 110, and 365 days. The median is 40. The mean is 97. Multiplying the median by seven gives 280, which understates the total badly. Multiplying the mean by seven gives 679, which is the actual sum.

Every operation there is fourth-grade arithmetic. What it shows is that multiplying by a mean does not claim that any particular person was held for the mean duration, and that multiplying a median by the number of people does not in general recover the total.

Other chapters do something similar. Chapter 3 fits a model of police call rates and compares its predictions against the observed counts. It then explains why the published analysis used Bayesian modeling, which supplied prior information about the likely range of call rates and steadied the estimates, since Williamstown has too few census block groups to pin them down from the data alone.

Chapter 7 concerns a risk score that courts use to predict whether someone will reoffend. It gives the reader two definitions of a fair score. One says the score should flag the same share of each racial group as high risk. The other says the score should be right equally often for each group. Both sound reasonable, and the chapter explains why satisfying one tends to cost you the other. Which of them should govern is unsettled in the fairness literature, and there is no arithmetic in the passage at all.

FourChange

Davis is right that the subtitle sets a demanding standard, and I accept the burden. “Change is hard and takes time,” my last chapter says, and “the world doesn’t magically improve simply because you or I publish research or share data” (p. 187). I conceded the difficulty before Davis raised it. But conceding it is not the same as coming up empty, and the review never takes up what is actually in the book.

Take the one Davis names himself. His summary credits me with creating JUSTFAIR, a database recording the judges for 600,000 federal sentences between 2001 and 2018. That is accurate, and it leaves out what the database changed. Before JUSTFAIR, the government’s public sentencing data did not identify the sentencing judges, and the scholarly work that had linked sentences to judges ran on proprietary data that forbade naming them.

JUSTFAIR is free and public. It pulled together information dispersed across several government sources, which no individual could realistically have assembled in consolidated form at this scale. Davis lists it among my projects and then concludes that little has changed, without saying why a public dataset that did not exist before does not count.

After JUSTFAIR went live, a couple of federal judges got in touch, wanting to examine their own sentencing patterns for bias because their court systems would not produce that analysis for them. My book calls it “one of the most optimism-inducing experiences of my professional life” (p. 140). I do not know what they did next.

The Chapter 4 letters are evidence of another kind. Judges do not write public letters about research nobody read.

Davis and I are counting different things. JUSTFAIR changed what the public can examine and what outsiders can analyze. Whether it changes how judges sentence is a separate question, and my book does not claim to have settled it. A reviewer who counts only sentencing should say so.

He writes that nothing in my book justifies the subtitle “The Power of Data to … Create Change.” The words inside that ellipsis are “Confront Inequity and” — half of what the subtitle claims, and the half the five projects he summarizes are about. He cut it from the quotation and then judged the book against what was left. A reader of the review has no way to know my subtitle mentions inequity at all.

The reviewer’s own standard

This is not the first Davis review to leave words out of a quotation. Reviewing Michael Harris’s Mathematics without Apologies for SIAM News in 2015, he quoted a sentence of Harris’s without its opening words, “It’s easy to argue that.” Harris pointed out that the cut turned a fictional character’s line into his own position.

Davis has written about what goes wrong when you study people whose beliefs you don’t share. In a 2014 essay arguing that social scientists treat conservatism and religion with contempt — he calls himself a dyed-in-the-wool liberal in it — he set out a rule. Start from respect. Presume not that the belief is true, but that it is not stupid. Otherwise, he warned, “Why do people believe X?” turns into “Why are people so stupid as to believe X?”

He wrote that about researchers rather than reviewers, but I think it carries over. Before you characterize what someone argued, reconstruct the argument they made.

My book asks the same of me. Chapter 2 argues that nobody analyzes from nowhere, that the questions we ask and the evidence we credit are shaped by where we stand, and that stating our premises is what lets someone else check us (p. 33). His rule and mine come to the same thing. Do not ask a reader to take your word for it.

Davis’s review breaks the rule twice. On objectivity, it attributes a position to me and cites nothing in my book that supports it. On change, it reaches a conclusion without saying what would have counted. Either way a reader is asked to trust a judgment without being shown what produced it.

Readers of the review will come away with the impression of a book in which Galton’s methods are irremediably tainted, questioning mathematical objectivity is an accusation about motives, the thinking is as simple as the arithmetic, and a decade of work has little to show.

I did not write that book.

Page references are to the first edition of Unlocking Justice (Princeton University Press, 2026). The review is Ernest Davis, “The Impact of Mathematics and Computation on Society,” SIAM News, Volume 59, Issue 7, September 1, 2026.
