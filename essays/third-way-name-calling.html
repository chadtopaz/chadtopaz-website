Third Way is hiring a director of name-calling

A $150,000 job to fight extremism turns out to target one wing of the Democratic Party — from an organization that once warned, in writing, against exactly this.

August 2026

The Washington think tank Third Way is hiring a “Director, Anti-Extremism.” The position pays $140,000 to $150,000 a year to “combat the rise of far-left extreme voices and forces.” The duties: assembling “a database of the Democratic Socialists of America’s most extreme ideas and statements” and keeping up “a drumbeat” of op-eds and television appearances. The posting sits alongside a $15 million campaign against the DSA, launched days after a progressive insurgent won Michigan’s Democratic Senate primary. The job title carries no modifier — anti-extremism, full stop. The full job description supplies the modifier, and of all the extremisms the title might cover, this position is built around one: the far left.

Democrats can argue in good faith about whether democratic socialism is smart politics. Coalitions fight over direction; that is what coalitions are for. But in American public life, “extremism” names two different things. One is ideological distance — ideas far from the mainstream. The other is political violence — the phenomenon that the FBI, civil rights organizations, and terrorism researchers define, track, and count. Third Way’s unmodified title borrows the gravity of the second for a campaign about the first.

Announcing the $15 million campaign, Third Way’s president, Jonathan Cowan, called the DSA a “mortal danger” — to the Democratic Party. Note the vocabulary: the language of existential threat, repurposed for a fight over the party’s direction. The posting works the same way. It gestures once at illiberal forces “no matter what side of the political spectrum they occupy.” But the job’s operational center of gravity — the DSA database, the polling, the spokespeople, the media drumbeat — points left. Third Way’s only advertised anti-extremism directorship is an opposition-research and communications campaign against the DSA and the candidates Third Way links to it.

I am a data scientist who works on issues of social justice, so when a label borrows the gravity of something measurable, I check the measurements. Start with lethality. The Center for Strategic and International Studies, no one’s idea of a leftist outfit, tracks terrorist attacks and plots, defined as premeditated violence for political ends. In the decade through mid-2025, left-wing attacks killed 13 people in the United States. Right-wing attacks killed 112. The Anti-Defamation League’s broader tally of extremist-related murders, a category that includes non-ideological killings by people with extremist ties, lands in the same place. In the decade through 2024, 76 percent of its 429 killings were tied to the far right. In February, the libertarian Cato Institute published a half-century accounting, one that stresses such violence is rare and its ideological shares volatile. Even so: set aside September 11 and the Oklahoma City bombing, and right-wing attacks still out-killed left-wing attacks nearly three to one.

These numbers cannot tell you whether the DSA’s ideas are good, popular, or even extreme. They tell you where the lethal kind of extremism lives — and it is not on the left.

And Third Way cannot claim confusion about the difference between a political opponent and a violent threat, because it once wrote the distinction down. In 2020, its national security program identified white supremacists and other far-right extremists as the country’s largest domestic-extremism threat. It criticized the Trump administration for fixating on antifa, a movement “not suspected of the same level of violence as far-right extremists.” That administration, Third Way wrote, was “more focused on partisan politics than in preventing and countering the real threats.” That was the standard: assess threats by the data, and do not inflate a lesser one for partisan gain. Six years later, Third Way is running the play it condemned: putting the broad language of extremism to partisan use against a left-wing adversary.

The posting also makes an electoral claim: “These extreme candidates still lose far more races than they win.” It never says which candidates, which races, or over what stretch of time — and the recent record does not cooperate. In the 2025 off-cycle elections, the DSA counted 14 wins and nine losses — the year New Yorkers elected democratic socialist Zohran Mamdani to run the nation’s largest city and Seattle elected a democratic socialist mayor of its own — Katie Wilson, whom the local DSA chapter declined to endorse. As of this writing, DSA’s own scoreboard for the 2026 cycle showed 63 wins against 54 losses, with 55 races still running. And on August 4, in the race that set off Third Way’s alarm, Abdul El-Sayed beat Representative Haley Stevens, a moderate, for Michigan’s Senate nomination. The DSA never endorsed El-Sayed; Cowan targets him anyway, as a practitioner of what he calls “the DSA dodge.” Whatever that record is, “losing far more races than they win” is not it.

Then there is the “vital center” Third Way says it defends. Political scientists estimate the ideological positions of members of Congress from their roll-call voting records, using a method called DW-NOMINATE. Pew Research’s summary of a half century of those estimates: from the early 1970s to the early 2020s, the average House Democrat moved from −0.31 to −0.38, a drift of 0.07. The average House Republican moved from 0.25 to 0.51 — nearly four times as far. The scholars who built the measure are blunt: polarization is “a Republican-led phenomenon.” So any center defined as the midpoint between the parties is moving, and it is moving rightward. Stand at that midpoint long enough and the people who barely moved start to look like radicals.

Third Way is free to spend $15 million fighting over the future of the Democratic Party. That is politics. But a broad anti-extremism title on a campaign against one wing of Democratic politics is not neutral description — least of all from an organization that warned, in writing, against inflating extremist threats for partisan purposes. Its mission statement promises “a critical bulwark against political extremism.” The job it is advertising is oppo research that labels the DSA extremist — along with whoever Third Way decides is close enough. That is not moderation. It is name-calling with a $15 million budget.

This is the kind of data-driven justice work I do in my book Unlocking Justice, now available from Princeton University Press.
