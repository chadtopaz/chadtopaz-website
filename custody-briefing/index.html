Overview

When parents separate, family courts can decide where their children live, who makes important decisions for them, and how the parents must communicate. These decisions become legally binding through court orders. In families affected by domestic violence, the conditions of continued contact are also a matter of safety. State law governs these decisions, but courts can also require professionals to follow privately developed standards and parents to use commercial services. [123–4]

Outside organizations participate in both custody-law debates and court practice. Advocacy groups promote changes in the law, professional associations supply standards, and companies supply services and proposed order language. This briefing documents how private standards and commercial services enter court requirements, then examines legal safeguards and evidence about family safety. Its court-practice examples focus on Ohio, with one California custody case. Its policy-statistics analysis identifies conflicting versions and measurement problems in Kentucky figures used to promote equal-time laws. [567–8]

One policy dispute concerns whether equal parenting time should be the legal starting point. The National Parents Organization campaigns for an equal-time presumption: a rule under which the court starts from the assumption that equal time with each parent is best for the child. A parent seeking a different arrangement must overcome that assumption with evidence or qualify for an exception. In Kentucky, this presumption does not apply in favor of a parent if a domestic-violence protection order has been issued against that parent to protect the other parent or a child in the case. Such an order imposes restrictions intended to protect against abuse. [5, 9]

Courts also regulate how parenting arrangements are carried out. They may appoint a parenting coordinator, a professional who helps parents follow an existing order and manage disputes. Ohio requires these professionals to follow the 2019 guidelines of the Association of Family and Conciliation Courts (AFCC), a private professional association whose members include judges, lawyers, and mental-health professionals. [3, 5, 10]

Court orders can also require parents to pay for a particular communication service. OurFamilyWizard sells an app for communication between separated parents and supplies model court orders: templates that set out requirements for using its product. A January 2023 Ohio order closely matches an older template distributed by the company. It required both parents to use the app and renew their subscriptions, generally making the app their exclusive channel for communication, with an emergency exception. The order identified a domestic-violence protection order then in effect and stated that using the app did not itself violate it. [4]

1. Custody Decisions and Family Safety

This section explains what custody orders decide, why the conditions of continued contact matter when abuse is involved, and which professionals advise courts or help implement orders. It then distinguishes allegations from judicial findings and examines a study of competing claims about abuse and parent-child relationships.

Custody and Parenting Time

Custody decisions address two questions that need not have the same answer: who makes decisions for the child, and where the child lives. State terminology varies; this briefing uses legal custody for decisions about upbringing, such as schooling and medical care, and physical custody or parenting time for where the child lives and when each parent cares for them. Parents can share decision-making without dividing residential time equally. A parenting arrangement allocates these responsibilities; an equal-time presumption supplies the legal starting point for deciding the schedule. [1]

Domestic Violence

These arrangements govern both children's contact with each parent and the parents' continuing involvement with one another. Domestic violence therefore matters to more than the number of days a child spends in each home. It can include sexual violence, stalking, threats, financial restriction, and coercive control: conduct used to monitor, intimidate, or dominate another person's choices. AFCC's guidance for professionals who evaluate custody arrangements recognizes that coercive control can occur without physical violence. It lists rules for parental communication, limits on information sharing, and neutral arrangements for handing children from one parent to the other as options for reducing opportunities for abuse after separation. [2]

In its 2023/2024 survey of U.S. adults, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated that 29.9% of women and 13.0% of men had experienced physical violence, contact sexual violence (including rape and unwanted sexual touching), or stalking by a current or former intimate partner during their lives, with an associated impact such as fear, injury, or need for services. These are national lifetime estimates, not estimates specifically for separating parents or custody litigants. [11]

Professional Assessments and Abuse Allegations

Judges issue custody orders, but other professionals may investigate a family's circumstances and make recommendations. In Ohio, a guardian ad litem, or GAL, investigates and recommends what would serve the child's best interests. A custody evaluator assesses the child's needs and the adults' capacities to meet them. These professionals collect information through interviews and records and present their assessments through written reports or testimony. [10]

Once an order is in place, a parenting coordinator can help implement it and resolve disputes within authority set by the appointment and governing rules. This implementation role is distinct from a GAL's investigation and recommendations. Courts may also order reunification interventions, services intended to repair a strained parent-child relationship. [7, 10]

In assessing a family's circumstances, professionals and judges may encounter competing accounts of abuse and parent-child relationships. An allegation is a claim that abuse occurred; a judicial finding is the court's determination after considering the evidence. An abuse allegation can be met with a claim that the reporting parent has improperly undermined the child's relationship with the other parent. This is a parental alienation allegation. Joan Meier's 2020 study of published U.S. court opinions from 2005-2014, mostly decisions on appeal, examined cases involving allegations of partner violence or physical or sexual abuse of children. [12]

The researchers counted a custody switch when a parent began with primary custody or physical care of the children and the court transferred custody to the other parent. Among mothers in the study who began with primary custody or physical care and alleged abuse by the father, custody switched to him in 50% of cases with a recorded alienation claim by the father, compared with 26% without one. A case counted as involving an alienation claim when the published opinion recorded the father's claim, whether or not the court accepted it. [12]

The study documents an association between these competing claims and custody outcomes in reported decisions. The researchers found that maternal custody losses were more common in the appealed cases than in the non-appealed cases they collected. The percentages therefore describe this selected sample, not the frequency of custody loss across custody cases generally. [12]

These findings are disputed. In a separate analysis of 967 appellate case reports involving alleged or professionally or judicially identified alienation, Jennifer Harman and Demosthenes Lorandos reported that their results did not support Meier's conclusions. They compared alleged alienation with alienation identified by an expert or court, rather than cases with and without an alienation claim. Meier and colleagues defended their methods in a 2022 response. The studies use different case selections and comparisons, which matters when interpreting their conflicting conclusions. A result from Meier's study also appears in the enacted findings for Kayden's Law, the federal custody-safety funding law discussed in Section 4. [1213–14]

2. The Organizations and Their Relationships

Professional associations provide guidance and education, while commercial providers sell services used in court cases. The first subsection introduces two professional associations and the app provider OurFamilyWizard; the second describes the company's financial, personnel, and educational relationships with professionals. The final subsection turns to a different activity: advocacy and professional positions on custody legislation. [5, 6, 15]

Organizations in Court Practice

AFCC brings together judges, lawyers, mental-health professionals, researchers, and court personnel. It publishes professional guidelines and offers education, including training for judges. [5]

The National Council of Juvenile and Family Court Judges (NCJFCJ), a nonprofit organization, provides judicial education and reference materials. [15]

OurFamilyWizard (OFW) sells subscriptions for parent-to-parent communication, scheduling, expense records, and document storage. Professional accounts let lawyers and other professionals access a connected parent's app information, including login IP-address histories. Access depends on the parent's authorization and whether one or both parents have connected their accounts to the professional. [16]

OFW's Professional and Organizational Relationships

OFW has both financial and personnel ties to AFCC. It was the sole Diamond sponsor listed for AFCC's 2019 conference and one of two in 2024. The 2024 prospectus advertised that tier at $10,000, including exhibit placement, promotion, and conference registrations, and marketed access to professionals responsible for selecting products and programs for courts, agencies, and firms. Bryan Altman moved from leadership at OFW to AFCC executive director in June 2024. AFCC's biography describes his earlier work on the company's educational partnership with the association. [17, 18]

NCJFCJ announced an OFW sponsorship and national education partnership in 2022, including plans for webinars and judicial reference materials. A 2025 guide on courtroom practices that account for the psychological effects of trauma carries both organizations' logos beneath the words "This bench card brought to you by" and lists two OFW representatives on its development and review committee. The card advises judges on recognizing trauma and writing orders, including no-contact orders and safe parent-child contact arrangements. [15]

The company supplies proposed language for court orders requiring its product. A December 2024 training program of the Shreveport, Louisiana, bar association included an OFW presenter and blank orders with selectable terms for subscriptions, professional access, and use of app records as evidence. A separate company resource kit invites professionals to use its model language for agreements or orders. [6]

OFW's professional-account terms, updated June 26, 2026, also permit professionals to buy discounted one-year parent-account coupons and either give them to clients free or resell them at a price the terms describe as "reasonable." These terms provide a commercial role for professionals in addition to their access to clients' app records. [16, 17]

Positions on Custody Law

National Parents Organization (NPO) campaigns for an equal-time presumption. Ohio Senate Bill 174 would replace the state's shared-parenting and sole-custody framework with court-approved parenting plans, without creating that presumption. In June 2026 testimony, NPO opposed the bill in part because it left judges to assess each case without an equal-time starting point. The Ohio Domestic Violence Network, a statewide coalition of domestic-violence organizations, supported the bill. It cited with approval an NCJFCJ model code calling for decisions based on the effects of abuse, risks to the abused parent and child, and the abusive parent's ability to meet the child's needs without continued abuse. [5, 19, 20]

An AFCC-convened expert group's 2014 report illustrates why support for shared parenting should not be equated with support for equal-time laws. A majority supported a presumption of joint decision-making but favored individualized parenting-time arrangements over a fixed time presumption. Several participants dissented, favoring a presumptive minimum amount of time with each parent. These are the positions recorded in that expert-group report, not a statement of AFCC's current organizational policy. [19]

3. How Private Standards and Services Become Court Requirements

The four examples below show different ways private standards and services enter court practice. Ohio incorporates a professional association's guidelines into statewide rules. The Abedrabbo order requires a paid app under terms matching a company-distributed model. Greene County publishes standard language for app orders on its court website. In a California case, a program's enrollment conditions became custody and contact restrictions. The resulting requirements concern both professional conduct and families' daily lives. [3, 4, 7, 21]

Example 1: Ohio's Adoption of AFCC Guidelines

Ohio's statewide court-administration rules require parenting coordinators to follow AFCC's 2019 parenting-coordination guidelines. Ohio's own rules take priority if the two conflict. The rules govern courts that use parenting coordination and the professionals conducting it. Courts may not use coordination to decide who holds custody under the order or to make substantive changes in parenting time. [3]

Ohio prohibits parenting coordination when domestic abuse or violence is alleged, suspected, or present unless all specified safeguards are met. These include ongoing screening, notice of the possible victim's right to decline, participation without fear of coercion or control, safety precautions, and procedures to end a session when abuse, violence, or coercion is threatened. AFCC's guidelines require coordinators to honor active protection orders, monitor safety, and suspend coordination when continuing is unsafe. [3]

Example 2: The OurFamilyWizard Order in Abedrabbo

In the Abedrabbo divorce case in Cuyahoga County, Ohio, a January 31, 2023 order required both parents to use OurFamilyWizard under detailed terms matching an older template distributed by the company. [4]

The father requested OFW use and a temporary parenting schedule in October 2022; the mother filed written oppositions to both. In a separate action asking the Supreme Court of Ohio to compel rulings on pending motions, the father said that the domestic-violence case prevented him from contacting the mother to arrange visitation and that the app would help facilitate it. On January 25, 2023, the Supreme Court required the parties to submit evidence in that action. Six days later, Judge Colleen Reali granted the requests for app use and alternating weeks of parenting time. The order states that it is consistent with the GAL's recommendation and that OFW use does not itself violate the existing protection order. [4, 22]

The signed order and the company-hosted template contain the same sixteen numbered app provisions in the same sequence. Thirteen provisions match in full after accounting for formatting, punctuation, and scanning differences. The other three contain case-specific protection-order information, selections for professional access, and notification settings. Selected requirements are summarized below. [4]

Selected requirement What the January 2023 order specified
Accounts and renewal Each parent must establish an OFW parent account within 14 days and include the ToneMeter add-on, which the order says is intended to encourage cordial and productive communication. Each must renew the annual subscription until the youngest child turns 18 or the court ends the requirement.
Communication and responses The parents must communicate through OFW instead of email, text, or telephone. Calls are permitted for child-related emergencies requiring action in less than 24 hours, and their subject and general content must be recorded in the app. Any entry requiring a response must be answered within 48 hours unless the entry permits longer.
Records, payments, and access Parents must use check-ins to record their presence at exchanges and visits, and the app's payment feature for reimbursements. The company describes check-ins as GPS-verified. The order requires access for each parent's attorney and checks the boxes for the GAL and a parenting coordinator.
Evidence in court The order says the court "accepts the stipulation of the parties" that app records may be admitted "without further foundation or objection." This language also appears in clause 15 of the company template.

At trial on February 6, six days after the order, the GAL, attorney Edward Jansen, testified that he did not consider the parents good candidates for shared parenting and had recommended designating the mother as residential parent and legal custodian, Ohio's designation for custody rights and responsibilities. He nevertheless supported equal parenting time, with alternating weeks and school exchanges to reduce the parents' direct contact. He recommended OFW use "as the Court has ordered" and expressed hope that observing and documenting communications would improve them. He described the child's bonds with both parents and largely appropriate care. Asked about the husband's domestic violence against the mother, he acknowledged a "significant documented history." [20, 22]

Example 3: Greene County's OurFamilyWizard Order Template

Greene County, Ohio, uses a compliance office to review attorney-drafted custody orders before filing. Its attorney information page supplies standard OFW language for cases in which the court orders the app or the parties request it. The saved document is a blank template, not a signed order in a particular case. [21]

The third paragraph of the Greene template would treat a failure to reply as consent or waiver if the message required a response and the receiving parent neither answered within 48 hours nor requested additional time. In ordinary terms, the proposed action could be treated as agreed to, or the parent's objection as given up. The clause expressly allows a request for more time to seek legal counsel or independent medical advice. [21]

Example 4: The Family Bridges Custody Order

In California's Johnston-Rossi case, the children lived primarily with their mother, who had obtained a domestic-violence restraining order against their father in 2015. The father had parenting time and telephone or video contact with the children. The appointed reunification therapist recommended more intensive therapy addressing their relationships with both parents. The appointed custody evaluator, Dr. Stan Katz, did not think a restrictive program such as Family Bridges, a provider of parent-child reunification services, was necessary for these children. [7]

In August 2021, a judge allowed the father to choose whether to enroll himself and the children in a week-long reunification program. According to the appellate court's account, the father then submitted Family Bridges paperwork and testified that enrollment required a court order covering the program's minimum 90-day follow-up period. Its conditions included no contact with the mother during that period. [7]

The mother opposed the longer program and sought to present live testimony. A different judge denied that request on the premise that the earlier judge had already resolved the substantive program issues. In December 2021, he ordered the children to participate in Family Bridges and live with their father during the program and its minimum 90-day follow-up period. The order generally prohibited contact with their mother, with exceptions for parental agreement or a further court order. [7]

A California appeals court paused enforcement during the appeal and reversed the order on January 30, 2023. It rejected the later judge's premise: the earlier order permitted a week-long program, not custody removal and contact restrictions lasting at least 90 days. The court found no evidence of changed circumstances supporting the custody change or adequate evidence that the disruption served the children's best interests. It also held that the mother had improperly been denied the opportunity to present live testimony. [7]

4. Legal Safeguards and Evidence About Family Outcomes

Legislatures have addressed the same safety issues illustrated by the court cases: how abuse affects custody decisions, which professionals may advise courts, and when courts may restrict a child's contact with a parent. This section explains Kentucky's safeguards and the federal funding conditions known as Kayden's Law, which do not themselves govern the Ohio and California orders described above. It then examines two types of outcome evidence: Kentucky statistics used to promote equal-time laws and research on parents' experiences with communication apps. [8, 9, 13, 23]

Custody Presumptions and Abuse Safeguards

Kentucky adopted an equal-time presumption for temporary orders in 2017 and for permanent orders in 2018. For permanent orders, the court starts from the position that joint custody and equal parenting time serve the child's best interests, subject to statutory exceptions and rebuttal by evidence. Ohio instead uses an individualized best-interest assessment and shared-parenting plans. SB174, discussed in Section 2, passed the Ohio Senate in November 2025 and remained in House Judiciary as of September 30, 2026; the latest listed hearing was June 10. [9, 20]

Kentucky's protection-order exception dates from 2018. Changes effective July 15, 2026 added requirements for considering abuse allegations and restrictions after findings of repeated abuse. Together, these provisions distinguish three circumstances:

  • An allegation of domestic violence or abuse, or child abuse or neglect, by one party against another party or their child must be considered before the other best-interest factors. The judge must make written findings about its impact, if any, on the child.
  • A domestic-violence protection order, including one previously entered, removes the favorable joint-custody and equal-time presumption for the restrained party when the order was obtained by the other party or on behalf of a child involved in the custody case.
  • A finding that a party committed two or more acts of domestic violence or abuse against the other party creates a rebuttable presumption against joint custody and equal time. The law also bars custody or unsupervised visits until that parent meets specified intervention, assessment, treatment, and supervised-visitation requirements, followed by the required court hearings. [9]

The 2026 law also makes a court finding of a malicious, knowingly baseless child-abuse allegation a custody factor; an allegation that is merely unproved does not meet that statutory description. Beginning January 1, 2027, paid visitation supervisors and parenting coordinators must complete 16 hours of initial training and eight hours annually, covering child development, domestic violence, risk, and related subjects. [9]

Professional Evidence and Reunification Orders

Kayden's Law, enacted federally in 2022, offers additional grant funding to states that adopt specified custody laws, professional standards, and training. Its conditions include requiring relevant expertise and clinical experience with abuse victims for professionals offering expert evidence about alleged abuse. Experience limited to evaluating cases for court does not satisfy that requirement. [13]

The required state laws must also prohibit removing a child from, or restricting contact with, a competent and protective parent who is not physically or sexually abusive and to whom the child is bonded or attached, solely to improve the child's relationship with the other parent. Court-ordered reunification treatment must have scientifically valid and generally accepted evidence of safety, effectiveness, and therapeutic value. Treatment premised on cutting off a child from a bonded or attached parent is prohibited. These provisions set the conditions for states to qualify for additional funding; their application in a state court depends on the state's own law. [13]

Kentucky's July 2026 law addresses custody transfers and contact restrictions after a court finds that one party committed domestic violence or abuse against the other party or a child of the parties. The court cannot remove custody from, or reduce the parenting time of, a competent, nonabusive parent to whom the child is bonded solely to improve the child's relationship with the offending parent. An order addressing the child's resistance to contact with the abusive parent must primarily address that parent's behavior. Kentucky's provision requires a prior finding of abuse; the federal funding conditions for comparable custody and contact restrictions do not. [9, 13]

The professional education described in Section 2 includes debate over these restrictions and their evidentiary basis. Kayden's Law's enacted findings include a statistic from the Meier study discussed in Section 1: courts accepted one of 51 child-sexual-abuse allegations against fathers in cases with paternal alienation claims. At a January 2025 AFCC webinar sponsored by OFW, presenters questioned reliance on "one study" and highlighted the limits of appellate-case samples. [12, 13, 24]

The presenters also criticized the law for giving insufficient attention to explanations for a child's resistance other than abuse, including parenting problems, parental absence, and alienating behavior. They welcomed greater education about domestic violence and child maltreatment and greater attention to alternatives to custody reversals. They called for screening for violence and abuse separately from assessing a child's resistance to contact. [24]

Evidence Cited for Equal-Time Laws

NPO argues that equal-time laws reduce domestic violence. Its June 2026 testimony to Ohio lawmakers cited child-maltreatment counts and domestic-violence case counts from several states. The analysis here examines one of those claims: a decline in Kentucky family-court cases linked to domestic-violence cases. NPO attributes the data to Kentucky's Administrative Office of the Courts. Its newer publication reports 979 cases in 2017 and 290 in 2024, a decline of about 70%, and its chart groups the counts by filing year. These are linked court records, not a direct measure of how often violence occurred. [8]

Two features of the published figures matter to the claim that the laws caused the decline. First, the earlier handout shows counts falling from 1,599 in 2011 to 988 in 2016, before either reform. Second, the newer publication gives higher values for every overlapping year, 2017-2022. The increases range from six cases for 2017 to 133 for 2022, when the count changes from 454 to 587. [8]

The larger revisions to recent years raise a specific question about the time allowed for cases to become linked. NPO describes the measure as the "subsequent incidence" of domestic-violence cases linked to family cases. If those links can be added after filing, recent family cases may have had less time to acquire one. The apparent decline could then partly reflect unequal follow-up time. Testing that explanation requires the original court extracts, linkage rules, extraction dates, and confirmation that the publications used comparable queries. [8]

Kentucky also publishes a broader, differently defined police-report series. Its 2025 domestic-violence report counts 32,904 electronic reports of domestic or dating violence in 2023, 32,960 in 2024, and 33,786 in 2025. These records cover police-documented events, not just families with linked court cases, and omit violence that was not reported or recorded. Because the populations and time periods differ, the police reports are a separate outcome series, not a like-for-like test of NPO's figures or the laws' effects. [25]

A separate argument is that equal-time laws discourage divorce. Stateline, a nonprofit news outlet, reported a 25% decline in Kentucky's divorce rate from 2016 to 2023, compared with 18% nationally. It quoted a researcher who studied the rates cautioning that the Kentucky decline could not be tied directly to the 2018 law because many factors influenced it. A declining divorce rate does not identify why individual marriages continued or whether those families became safer. [26]

Communication Apps and Continued Contact

A 2024 study examined mothers' experiences of co-parenting apps in the context of reported abusive, controlling, or violent conduct. The anonymous survey recruited users through app developers, organizations serving separated parents, and Facebook advertising in five countries, including the United States. From 140 responses, the study selected 33 mothers who reported such conduct through an app, still used an app, and could be classified into one of two outcome groups. The paper does not identify the apps the mothers used, and the study was not limited to parents ordered by a court to use an app. [23]

The classification used reported changes in conflict, stress, perceived safety, and how complicated daily life felt. Twenty-four mothers reported improvement on at least one measure and worsening on none; nine reported worsening on at least one and improvement on none. Former users were not asked the follow-up questions, and current users with mixed, unchanged, or missing outcomes were excluded. The 24-to-nine split therefore describes these selected groups, not a success rate for app users generally. [23]

The groups also differed in their circumstances. Mothers reporting worsening outcomes had been separated for an average of 6.9 years, compared with three years in the improvement group. They reported more conflict and app misuse and were more likely to describe the relationship as fearful. The author interpreted these differences as a warning about using apps where coercive control is entrenched. She also noted that excluding former users may favor the experiences of mothers who found apps helpful. [23]

In their written comments, mothers described useful evidence, fewer unwanted interactions, and greater control over when and how to communicate. They also described false or misleading messages added to the record, monitoring, pressure to respond, and distress from continued contact. Some mothers reported less harassment or greater control over communication even though abusive messages continued. These accounts concern the communication and record-keeping functions required by the orders in Section 3. [23]

An Ohio case separately documents a parent's effort to maintain an app-only communication boundary. In State v. Hawks, the parenting agreement required OFW, and the mother repeatedly told her former husband to stop contacting her cellphone and use the app. He continued the cellphone contact after police told him to stop. The appeals court upheld his telecommunications-harassment convictions for knowingly communicating after requests to stop; the convictions concerned that unwanted contact rather than simply violating the parenting agreement. [27]

5. Conclusion

The findings support three lines of investigation: measuring the extent of private involvement in court practice, examining how courts assess professional recommendations and service requirements when abuse is involved, and testing the evidence used to promote custody laws.

Private involvement includes professional education as well as binding court requirements. OurFamilyWizard sponsors professional organizations and is credited on judicial guidance. Ohio incorporates guidelines from AFCC, a private professional association, into its parenting-coordination rules. The Abedrabbo order requires paid OFW subscriptions under terms matching a company-distributed template; Greene County distributes language that would treat some unanswered app messages as consent or waiver. In Johnston-Rossi, a provider's program conditions became custody and contact restrictions that an appeals court later overturned. [3, 4, 7, 15, 17, 21]

To establish the scale of this involvement, we should inventory Ohio court rules and standard forms, then sample custody cases from courts both with and without published requirements. This would distinguish courts that distribute private terms from cases in which judges actually impose them. The inquiry should record who approves the terms, what families must buy, and what alternatives or objections are available. Professional standards, commercial services, and sponsorship or training relationships should be counted separately. These are questions about the use of public authority and compulsory spending, whether or not a service improves family outcomes.

The effects of particular requirements on family safety are a separate question. Mothers in the 2024 parenting-app study reported useful records and fewer unwanted interactions, but also monitoring and pressure to respond. Some experienced benefits while abuse continued: one mother said the app helped protect her other communication channels, even though her former partner still used the app abusively. [23]

To follow up on this issue, we should examine how courts evaluate professional recommendations and service requirements when abuse is involved. This includes whether they apply the relevant safety safeguards, assess restrictions on family contact, and respond to parents' objections. In Abedrabbo, the underlying filings could identify who proposed the detailed app terms, how the court considered the mother's opposition, and the basis for the stated agreement to admit app records. In cases using Greene County's language, we should examine how courts apply consent-by-nonresponse terms when a parent raises a safety concern. Comparing these records with parenting-coordination and reunification cases would show how safety concerns are handled across services. [3, 4, 7, 21, 22]

The Kentucky linked-case statistics used to promote equal-time laws do not directly measure violence. NPO cites their decline as evidence that Kentucky's laws reduced violence. Yet counts were falling before the 2017 and 2018 reforms, and the newer publication raises every overlapping year's count, especially recent years. That pattern makes the timing and rules for linking records an important part of evaluating the claim. [8]

To follow up on this issue, we should examine whether the Kentucky evidence supports the claim that equal-time laws make families safer. The first step is to obtain the original court extracts and determine whether links can be added after filing and whether each filing year has the same follow-up period. We should also compare consistent measures of abuse and its consequences before and after reform, accounting for preexisting trends and changes in reporting. The testimony's other state and child-maltreatment comparisons require their own checks. Fewer court cases cannot, by themselves, tell us that families experienced less violence. [8]

Together, these investigations could inform proposals about disclosure and review of private requirements, families' ability to seek different terms, and the evidence legislators use to assess custody reforms.

Pp. 8-10: benefits and harms in both groups; pp. 11-12: interpretation and possible bias from excluding former users. Apps used are not identified. Recruitment covered five countries; final country counts are not reported. Not limited to court-ordered users. Online source.
